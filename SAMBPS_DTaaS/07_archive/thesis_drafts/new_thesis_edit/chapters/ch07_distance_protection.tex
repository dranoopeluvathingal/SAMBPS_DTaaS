\chapter{Distance Protection for Inverter-Dominated Networks}
\label{ch:distance}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ch5_intro}

Distance protection (ANSI 21) is the workhorse of transmission and
sub-transmission line protection worldwide. Its fundamental advantage—
measurement of impedance rather than current alone—makes it inherently
more selective than overcurrent protection. However, as shown in
Chapter~\ref{ch:ibr_char} (failures F4--F6), IBR reactive current
injection and current limitation both distort the apparent impedance
measurement, causing overreach, underreach, and directional loss.

This chapter addresses these failures through two complementary contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C5}: A quadrilateral distance characteristic with explicit
      IBR reach correction (Section~\ref{sec:ch5_quadrilateral}).
\item \textbf{C6}: A Monte Carlo reliability framework that quantifies
      the probability of correct operation across the full
      IBR operating envelope (Section~\ref{sec:ch5_reliability}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Classical Distance Relay Review}
\label{sec:ch5_review}

\subsection{Mho Characteristic}

The mho (admittance circle) is the most widely used Zone~1 characteristic.
In the complex $Z$-plane, the mho circle passes through the origin and
is centred at $Z_c = Z_\mathrm{reach}/2$ with radius $|Z_\mathrm{reach}|/2$:
\begin{equation}
  |Z_\mathrm{measured} - Z_c| \leq \frac{|Z_\mathrm{reach}|}{2}
  \label{eq:ch5_mho}
\end{equation}
Equivalently in phasor algebra, using the polarising voltage $\phasor{V}_P$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Re}\!\left[\phasor{I}_A \cdot \overline{Z_\mathrm{reach}} \cdot
    \overline{\phasor{V}_P}\right] \geq |\phasor{V}_P|^2 / |Z_\mathrm{reach}|
  \label{eq:ch5_mho_phasor}
\end{equation}
The mho characteristic has two key limitations for IBR networks:
\begin{itemize}
\item It cannot accommodate resistive faults: the mho circle has zero
      width at the origin, so high-resistance faults ($R_F > 0$) fall
      outside the characteristic.
\item It is sensitive to the reactive current injection: the reactive
      component of $\phasor{I}_B$ shifts $Z_\mathrm{measured}$ off the
      fault impedance locus.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Zone Structure}

Classical three-zone distance protection provides:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Zone 1}: Instantaneous trip for faults within 80--85\% of
      the line ($\alpha \leq 0.8$) to avoid overreach into Zone~1 of
      the next line.
\item \textbf{Zone 2}: Delayed trip (200--300~ms) for faults within
      120--150\% of the line, covering the remote bus and part of
      the adjacent lines.
\item \textbf{Zone 3}: Reverse-looking backup for internal bus faults;
      or forward-looking with 500~ms time delay.
\end{itemize}

\section{IBR Impact on Apparent Impedance}
\label{sec:ch5_ibr_impact}

\subsection{General Infeed Equation}

From Chapter~\ref{ch:ibr_char}, the apparent impedance with IBR infeed is:
\begin{equation}
  Z_\mathrm{app} = \alpha Z_L + Z_F\left(1 + \frac{\phasor{I}_B}{\phasor{I}_A}\right)
  \label{eq:ch5_zapp_general}
\end{equation}
The infeed ratio $\rho_I = \phasor{I}_B/\phasor{I}_A$ depends on:
\begin{itemize}
\item The pre-fault load current $I_L$ and power factor angle $\phi_L$.
\item The fault location $\alpha$.
\item The IBR current limit $I_\mathrm{lim}$ and commanded angle $\phi_\mathrm{cmd}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phase Angle Effect}

The commanded angle $\phi_\mathrm{cmd}$ of the IBR has a decisive effect.
Let $\phasor{I}_A = I_A\angle 0^\circ{}$ and $\phasor{I}_B = \kibr I_r \angle \beta$
where $\beta = \phi_\mathrm{cmd} + \delta_\mathrm{PLL}$. Then:
\begin{equation}
  Z_\mathrm{app} = \alpha Z_L + Z_F
    + Z_F \kibr \frac{I_r}{I_A} e^{\jj\beta}
  \label{eq:ch5_zapp_phase}
\end{equation}
The reactive component of $Z_\mathrm{app}$ is shifted by:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta X = Z_F \kibr \frac{I_r}{I_A} \sin\beta
  \label{eq:ch5_dx}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\beta = 0$ (unity power factor): $\Delta X = 0$; pure resistive
      shift along the $R$-axis (underreach for mho, manageable).
\item $\beta = +90^\circ{}$ (leading reactive): $\Delta X = +Z_F\kibr I_r/I_A$;
      positive $X$ shift → apparent impedance moves above the fault locus
      → \textbf{underreach}.
\item $\beta = -90^\circ{}$ (lagging reactive): $\Delta X = -Z_F\kibr I_r/I_A$;
      negative $X$ shift → apparent impedance moves into Zone~1
      for $\alpha > 0.8$ → \textbf{overreach}.
\end{itemize}

The direction of reactive injection is determined by IEEE~2800 Volt-VAr
control mode: absorbing reactive power during a nearby fault (where
$V_\mathrm{PCC}$ dips) sets $\beta = -90^\circ{}$ (lagging), causing overreach.
This is the most common IBR fault scenario and the most dangerous for
distance relay security.

\section{Quadrilateral Distance Characteristic for IBR}
\label{sec:ch5_quadrilateral}

\subsection{Quadrilateral Geometry}

The quadrilateral characteristic provides independent control over
the reactive ($X$) and resistive ($R$) reach limits:
\begin{align}
  X_1 &\leq X_\mathrm{relay} \leq X_\mathrm{reach} & \text{(reactance reach)}
  \label{eq:ch5_quad_x} \\
  R_1 &\leq R_\mathrm{relay} \leq R_\mathrm{reach} & \text{(resistance reach)}
  \label{eq:ch5_quad_r} \\
  R_\mathrm{relay} &\geq \tan(\angle Z_L - \delta_\mathrm{load}) \cdot X_\mathrm{relay}
    & \text{(load exclusion boundary)}
  \label{eq:ch5_quad_load}
\end{align}
The quadrilateral operates when all three conditions are met:
\begin{equation}
  T_{21} = \mathbf{1}[C_X \cap C_R \cap C_\mathrm{load}]
  \label{eq:ch5_quad_trip}
\end{equation}
The resistance reach $R_\mathrm{reach}$ can be set independently to
accommodate high-impedance fault resistance without extending the $X$ reach.

\subsection{IBR-Corrected Reactance Measurement}

The classical reactance measurement uses $V_a - I_a Z_L$, which is
corrupted by reactive current injection. The IBR-corrected reactance
measurement is:
\begin{equation}
  X_\mathrm{corr} = \frac{\mathrm{Im}\!\left[(\phasor{V}_a
    - \alpha_\mathrm{est} Z_L \phasor{I}_a) \cdot
    \overline{\phasor{I}_a}\right]}{|\phasor{I}_a|^2}
  \label{eq:ch5_xcorr}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_\mathrm{est}$ is the estimated fault location from the
digital twin (Chapter~\ref{ch:digital_twin}, equation \ref{eq:ch7_ekf_alpha}).
Substituting $\alpha_\mathrm{est}$ removes the reactive current term
from the measurement. The residual error is:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta X_\mathrm{residual} = Z_F \kibr \frac{I_r}{I_A}
    \sin(\beta - \angle Z_F) \cdot |\alpha - \alpha_\mathrm{est}|
  \label{eq:ch5_xcorr_error}
\end{equation}
For RMSE $|\alpha - \alpha_\mathrm{est}| \leq 0.011$ (EKF performance,
Section~\ref{sec:ch7_ekf_results}) and $Z_F = 10$~$\Omega$,
$\kibr = 0.5$: $\Delta X_\mathrm{residual} \leq 0.055$~$\Omega$ (negligible).

\subsection{Adaptive Quadrilateral Reach Correction}

The Zone~1 reactive reach is corrected for IBR infeed as:
\begin{equation}
  X_\mathrm{reach,corr} = X_\mathrm{reach} - k_X \kibr \frac{I_\mathrm{rated}}{I_A}
  \label{eq:ch5_xreach_corr}
\end{equation}
where $k_X$ is the reactive correction coefficient, set as:
\begin{equation}
  k_X = |Z_F^\mathrm{max}| \cdot |\sin\beta_\mathrm{max}|
  \approx 10\,\Omega \cdot 1 = 10\,\Omega
  \label{eq:ch5_kx}
\end{equation}
This correction prevents Zone~1 overreach for lagging reactive injection
while maintaining full coverage for unity power factor IBR operation.

\subsection{Cross-Polarised Mho Element}

The cross-polarised mho uses a healthy-phase voltage as the polarising
reference, eliminating the memory-voltage dependency:
\begin{equation}
  \phasor{V}_\mathrm{pol} = \phasor{V}_b - \phasor{V}_c
    \quad\text{(for phase~A fault)}
  \label{eq:ch5_crosspol}
\end{equation}
The cross-polarised operate condition is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Re}\!\left[\phasor{I}_a \overline{Z_\mathrm{reach}}
    \cdot \overline{\phasor{V}_\mathrm{pol}}\right] \geq 0
  \label{eq:ch5_crosspol_operate}
\end{equation}
This eliminates failure mode F6 (directional loss after memory expiry)
because the healthy-phase voltage $\phasor{V}_{bc}$ remains near rated
for single-phase and double-phase faults.

\section{Zone~2 POTT Communication Scheme}
\label{sec:ch5_pott}

Zone~2 coverage is extended by the Permissive Overreaching Transfer Trip
(POTT) scheme over IEC~61850 GOOSE:
\begin{enumerate}
\item End~A measures $Z_\mathrm{app}$ in Zone~2: asserts POTT-permissive
      GOOSE bit.
\item End~B receives GOOSE and checks: (a) Zone~2 qualified, and
      (b) directional element forward.
\item If both conditions met: End~B asserts instantaneous trip.
\end{enumerate}
The GOOSE trip latency is $\leq 4$~ms (IEC~61850 performance class P1).
The total end-to-end trip time for Zone~2 faults is $4 + t_\mathrm{CT} +
t_\mathrm{meas} \leq 8$~ms (one cycle).

\subsection{POTT Security Logic for IBR}

A conventional POTT scheme can send a spurious permissive when the IBR
reactive current injection causes Zone~2 overreach during a through-fault.
The IBR-secure POTT adds a check on the IBR current ratio:
\begin{equation}
  T_\mathrm{POTT} = T_{Z2,A} \cap T_{Z2,B} \cap
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[\kibr < 0.45\right]
  \label{eq:ch5_pott_ibr}
\end{equation}
For $\kibr > 0.45$, the Zone~2 reach is reduced to 110\% and the POTT
timer is extended to 100~ms (versus the instantaneous trip), providing
additional time for the directional element to stabilise.

\section{Zero-Sequence Compensation and Ground Distance}
\label{sec:ch5_ground_distance}

\subsection{Zero-Sequence Compensation Factor}

The ground distance element must compensate for the different propagation
of zero- and positive-sequence currents:
\begin{equation}
  Z_\mathrm{gnd} = \frac{V_a}{I_a + k_0 \cdot 3I_0}
  \label{eq:ch5_znd_comp}
\end{equation}
The compensation factor $k_0$ is:
\begin{equation}
  k_0 = \frac{Z_{L,0} - Z_{L,1}}{3 Z_{L,1}}
  \label{eq:ch5_k0}
\end{equation}
For underground cables: $k_0 \approx 2$ (overhead lines: $k_0 \approx 0.5$--$1.0$).

\subsection{IBR Impact on Ground Distance}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:ch3_challenges}, $I_0^\mathrm{IBR} = 0$
for three-wire converters. The ground distance element at end~A therefore
measures:
\begin{equation}
  Z_\mathrm{gnd} = \frac{V_{a,A}}{I_{a,A} + k_0 \cdot 3I_{0,\mathrm{system}}}
  \label{eq:ch5_znd_ibr}
\end{equation}
where $I_{0,\mathrm{system}}$ is the zero-sequence current from the
grid infeed only. If the fault is at $\alpha = 1$ (remote end) on an
IBR-only line, $I_{0,\mathrm{system}} \to 0$ and $Z_\mathrm{gnd} \to \infty$.

\textbf{Mitigation}: A grounding transformer (ZN or Z) at the IBR substation
provides a zero-sequence source. The grounding transformer impedance
$Z_{g}$ must be included in $k_0$:
\begin{equation}
  k_0^\mathrm{eff} = k_0 + \frac{Z_g}{3 Z_{L,1}}
  \label{eq:ch5_k0_eff}
\end{equation}
Setting $Z_g = 3Z_\mathrm{req}$ where $Z_\mathrm{req}$ is chosen to limit
ground fault current to $I_0^\mathrm{max}$ while ensuring $Z_\mathrm{gnd}$
stays within Zone~1 reach for SLG faults at $\alpha \leq 0.8$.

\section{Auto-Reclosing Strategy for IBR Lines}
\label{sec:ch5_autoreclosing}

\subsection{IBR Hazard: Out-of-Phase Reclosing}

Classical auto-reclosing (ANSI~79) assumes that after a trip, the circuit
voltage collapses on the tripped side. With IBR ride-through, the voltage
does not collapse: the IBR continues to energise the tripped section.
Reclosing into an energised section with a phase angle difference
$\Delta\theta > 30^\circ{}$ causes a severe electromagnetic torque surge.

\subsection{Adaptive Reclosing Logic}

The adaptive reclosing scheme monitors:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Line-side voltage $V_L$ after breaker open: if $V_L > 0.2$~pu, IBR
      is still energising → inhibit reclose until $V_L < 0.2$~pu.
\item Phase angle difference $|\theta_S - \theta_L|$: if $> 30^\circ{}$ → inhibit.
\item Frequency difference $|f_S - f_L|$: if $> 0.5$~Hz → inhibit.
\end{enumerate}
The reclose is permitted when all three conditions clear simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
  T_\mathrm{reclose} = \mathbf{1}\!\left[V_L < 0.2\right]
    \cap \mathbf{1}\!\left[|\theta_S - \theta_L| < 30^\circ{}\right]
    \cap \mathbf{1}\!\left[|f_S - f_L| < 0.5\,\mathrm{Hz}\right]
  \label{eq:ch5_reclose}
\end{equation}
The maximum waiting time before lockout is 5~s; if IBR does not
de-energise within 5~s, the breaker is locked out and a DTT signal is
sent to the IBR unit via GOOSE to force a controlled shutdown.

\section{Monte Carlo Reliability Framework}
\label{sec:ch5_reliability}

\subsection{Framework Design}

scheme reliability across the statistical distribution of IBR operating points.

\subsection{Reliability Metrics}

The framework computes three reliability indices for each scheme:
\begin{align}
  P_\mathrm{detect} &= P(\text{trip} | \text{internal fault})
  \label{eq:ch5_pdetect} \\
  P_\mathrm{secure} &= P(\text{no trip} | \text{external fault or load})
  \label{eq:ch5_psecure} \\
  P_\mathrm{coord} &= P(\text{primary clears before backup})
  \label{eq:ch5_pcoord}
\end{align}
A scheme is considered \emph{reliable} if $P_\mathrm{detect} \geq 0.99$,
$P_\mathrm{secure} \geq 0.999$, and $P_\mathrm{coord} \geq 0.995$.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch5_summary}

This chapter has developed distance protection for IBR-dominated networks:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The apparent impedance shift caused by IBR reactive current injection
      is fully characterised by Equations~\eqref{eq:ch5_zapp_general}--\eqref{eq:ch5_dx}.
\item The IBR-corrected quadrilateral (C5) eliminates overreach by
      subtracting the reactive shift, and extends resistive coverage to
      $R_F \leq 100$~$\Omega$.
\item Cross-polarised mho eliminates directional loss (failure mode F6).
\item The POTT scheme with IBR current ratio inhibit provides 8~ms
      Zone~2 clearing while preventing false trips for $\kibr > 0.45$.
\item Adaptive reclosing prevents out-of-phase closing into IBR-sustained
      line voltage.
\item The Monte Carlo framework (C6) provides a statistically rigorous
      reliability quantification across the full IBR operating envelope.
\end{enumerate}
