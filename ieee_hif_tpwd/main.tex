\begin{abstract}
Conventional overcurrent (OC) relays for synchronous-generator (SG) feeders
are poorly suited to high-impedance faults (HIFs), where the fault current
falls below the instantaneous threshold and the inverse-time element governs
clearance.  Existing adaptive schemes focus primarily on pickup adaptation
under low-impedance conditions and do not quantify how online source-parameter
estimation should modify the time-multiplier setting (TMS) in the HIF regime.
This paper introduces an impedance-aware adaptive TMS law driven directly by
online reduced-order inverse estimation of SG source parameters identified via
a two-pass Levenberg--Marquardt (LM) algorithm.  Four contributions are made:
(i)~a rigorous fault-regime classification that analytically separates the
instantaneous, inverse-time, and no-trip regions as $\Rf$ increases, with
closed-form boundary expressions derived from the machine subtransient model;
(ii)~an impedance-aware TMS adaptation law derived from the estimated
steady-state current $\Iss$, incorporating a resistance-aware correction factor
$\fhif$ and a regime-dependent effective gain $\Keff$, with a formal
proposition bounding the TMS reduction; (iii)~demonstration that the adaptive
benefit becomes operationally meaningful \emph{only} when the inverse-time
element governs, closing a key limitation of the prior Stage~1 study; and
(iv)~full compatibility with the existing confidence-gated bounded-update
inverse-estimation framework, preserving protection-grade real-time operation
throughout the HIF window.
Simulation results over $\Rf = 20$--$500\,\Omega$ on a 10~MVA
synchronous-generator feeder demonstrate that: two regimes are correctly
identified with analytical boundary agreement; adaptive TMS reduces clearance
time by \textbf{17--23\%} in the inverse-time window; missed-trip boundaries
are unchanged; confidence acceptance exceeds \textbf{89\%} (3PH) and
\textbf{100\%} (SLG); and no security
degradation (false trips) is observed across 144 simulation cases.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

\IEEEPARstart{O}{vercurrent} (OC) protection remains the primary or backup
protection function for synchronous-generator (SG) feeders and active
distribution networks with embedded generation~\cite{Anderson1995,Blackburn2006}.
In practice, a relay combines a high-set instantaneous element with an
IEC~60255 inverse-time element to achieve fast clearance for bolted faults
and selective, graded operation at reduced current levels.  Fixed settings for
both elements are calibrated at the planning stage under a worst-case impedance
scenario, making them inherently conservative when source conditions depart
from that scenario.

The limitation becomes critical under high-impedance faults (HIFs), which
arise from vegetation contact, dry-surface broken conductors, corroded
connections, and progressive insulation degradation~\cite{Lazkano2000,
Ghaderi2017,Wester1998}.  A HIF inserts a resistive path $\Rf$ that depresses
the fault current well below the instantaneous pickup threshold,
forcing the relay into inverse-time operation.  Here, the clearance speed
is governed \emph{entirely} by the TMS --- a setting calibrated for
bolted-fault conditions where large current multiples make the TMS
irrelevant to protection speed.  The result can be multi-second or
even missed clearance, increasing arc duration, thermal damage,
and fire risk.

\subsection{Limitations of the State of the Art}

Adaptive OC protection has attracted sustained research since Novosel
\emph{et al.}~\cite{Novosel1994} demonstrated dynamic fault-current
adaptation.  Subsequent work has shown effective real-time adjustment
of pickup current $I_p$ under changing generation dispatch and network
topology~\cite{Sharaf2011,Saleh2017,Darlington2021}.  Model-based
inverse-estimation schemes identify source parameters from post-fault
measurements and map them to bounded relay updates~\cite{Eluvathingal2025_TR01,
Eluvathingal2026_TR02}.

However, a consistent gap runs through the literature: virtually all adaptive
pickup or TMS work is validated on low-impedance, high-current faults where
the instantaneous element dominates.  In that regime, adaptive TMS has
limited practical value because clearance time is short regardless.
The inverse-time operating regime --- precisely where HIF places the relay
--- has received comparatively little attention from an online adaptation
standpoint.  Specifically, \emph{no prior work has derived an adaptive TMS
law from online source-parameter estimation and demonstrated its benefit
across the full HIF inverse-time window}.

The Stage~1 study~\cite{Eluvathingal2025_TR01} established that reduced-order
source parameters ($I_{ss}$, $I_{sub}$, $\tau_{ac}$) can be identified online
with $R^2 > 0.999$ waveform fit and confidence $\gamma > 0.89$ for bolted-fault
cases, and that the estimates support bounded adaptive pickup updates.  However,
Stage~1 simulations were predominantly in the instantaneous regime
($\Rf \leq 15\,\Omega$), so adaptive pickup improved security without
materially changing trip time.  High-impedance faults are the correct next
milestone because they shift the relay into the inverse-time region and make
TMS adaptation the decisive intervention.

\subsection{Paper Contributions}

This paper extends inverse-estimation-based adaptive OC protection from the
instantaneous operating region into the inverse-time HIF regime by introducing
an impedance-aware TMS mapping derived directly from online reduced-order
source-parameter estimates.  The four specific contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A rigorous fault-regime classification for SG OC protection that
      analytically separates the instantaneous, inverse-time, and no-trip
      regimes as $\Rf$ increases, with closed-form boundary expressions.
      (\textit{Section~\ref{sec:regime}})

\item An impedance-aware TMS adaptation law incorporating a resistance-aware
      correction factor $\fhif(\Iss)$ and a regime-dependent effective gain
      $\Keff$, with a formal proposition bounding the TMS reduction in the
      HIF regime and a proof that bounded updates preserve minimum grading margins.
      (\textit{Section~\ref{sec:tmslaw}})

\item Numerical demonstration that adaptive benefit is strictly confined to
      the inverse-time regime and is absent in the instantaneous regime,
      closing the main limitation of Stage~1.
      (\textit{Section~\ref{sec:results}})

\item Proof of full compatibility with the confidence-gated reduced-order
      inverse-estimation framework, including correct security behaviour
      (gate blocking near the no-trip boundary).
      (\textit{Sections~\ref{sec:estimation}--\ref{sec:tmslaw}})
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:sgmodel} summarises the synchronous-machine fault model.
Section~\ref{sec:regime} formulates the HIF regime classification.
Section~\ref{sec:baseline} analyses the limitation of a fixed TMS.

Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the study system.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Synchronous-Generator Fault Model}
\label{sec:sgmodel}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Subtransient Short-Circuit Current}
\label{sec:sgmodel:sub}

For a three-phase SG connected through an external impedance to a fault
through resistance $\Rf$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_system}), the peak subtransient
current at fault inception $(t = t_0^+)$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\hat{I}_{\mathrm{sub}} =
  \frac{\sqrt{2}\,V_0}{\bigl|(Z_d'' + Z_{\mathrm{th}} + \Rf)\bigr|},
\label{eq:Isub_exact}
\end{equation}
where $V_0$ is the pre-fault terminal voltage (p.u.),
$Z_d'' = R_a + jX_d''$ is the machine subtransient impedance, and
$Z_{\mathrm{th}} = R_{\mathrm{th}} + jX_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the Thévenin
feeder impedance.  The DC offset at inception depends on the inception angle
$\phi_a$ via $I_{DC,0} = -\hat{I}_{\mathrm{sub}}\sin(\phi_a)$.

\subsection{Analytical Fault-Current Synthesis}
\label{sec:sgmodel:synthesis}

Following~\cite{IEEE1110}, the phase-A fault current is accurately
approximated by a three-component reduced-order model over the first
150--300~ms following fault inception:
\begin{align}
i_a(t) &= \underbrace{%
  \bigl[I_{ss} + (I_{sub} - I_{ss})\,e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{ac}}\bigr]
  \sin(\omega t + \phi_a)}_{\text{AC component}}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad
  - \underbrace{%
  I_{sub}\sin(\phi_a)\,e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{dc}}}_{\text{DC offset}},
\label{eq:ia}
\end{align}
where $t_{\mathrm{rel}} = t - t_0$, $I_{ss}$ is the steady-state
(synchronous) fault current, $I_{sub} = \hat{I}_{\mathrm{sub}}/\sqrt{2}$
is the subtransient peak (RMS equivalent), $\tau_{ac}$ is the AC envelope
decay time constant, and $\tau_{dc}$ is the DC decay time constant.

The key parameter relationships are~\cite{Kundur1994,IEEE1110}:
\begin{align}
I_{ss}    &= \frac{V_0}{|X_d + X_{\mathrm{th}} + \Rf|},
\label{eq:Iss} \\
\tau_{ac} &\approx \frac{X_d'}{R_a + R_{\mathrm{th}}}\,\cdot\,
                   \frac{T_{d0}'}{2\pi f_0},
\label{eq:tauac}\\
\tau_{dc} &= \frac{X_d'' + X_{\mathrm{th}}}{\omega\,(R_a + R_{\mathrm{th}} + \Rf)}.
\label{eq:taudc}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Iss} is the fundamental link exploited in this paper:
$I_{ss}$ decreases monotonically with $\Rf$, making it a natural proxy for
the fault resistance and the basis for the adaptive TMS correction factor.

The six-parameter vector is
$\bm{\theta} = [t_0,\,I_{ss},\,I_{sub},\,\tau_{ac},\,\tau_{dc},\,\phi_a]^\top
  \in \mathbb{R}^6$,
with bounds imposed by machine nameplate data and physics
(Table~\ref{tab:system}).

\section{HIF Operating-Regime Classification}
\label{sec:regime}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Current Multiple and Regime Boundaries}
\label{sec:regime:boundaries}

Define the current multiple
\begin{equation}
M = \frac{I_{\mathrm{rms}}}{I_p},
\qquad
I_{\mathrm{rms}} \approx \frac{\hat{I}_{\mathrm{sub}}}{\sqrt{2}},
\label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
where $I_p$ is the inverse-time pickup current.  Three regimes arise as
$\Rf$ increases from zero (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}):

\begin{description}
\item[Instantaneous regime ($M \geq M_{\mathrm{inst}}$):] The high-set
     element operates within one cycle.  TMS is irrelevant; adaptive pickup
     governs security.

\item[Inverse-time regime ($1 < M < M_{\mathrm{inst}}$):] The instantaneous
     element does not operate; the inverse-time element integrates toward
     a trip.  Both $I_p$ and TMS affect clearance speed.
     \emph{This is the governing regime for HIF operation.}

\item[No-trip regime ($M \leq 1$):] The relay does not pick up.
     OC protection fails; directional earth-fault or sensitive ground
     elements are required.
\end{description}

\noindent
The exact instantaneous-to-inverse-time boundary in $\Rf$ is found by
setting $\hat{I}_{\mathrm{sub}} = I_{\mathrm{inst}}$ (peak instantaneous
threshold) in~\eqref{eq:Isub_exact}:
\begin{equation}
R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}} =
  \sqrt{\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\,V_0}{I_{\mathrm{inst}}}\right)^{\!2}
  - (X_d'' + X_{\mathrm{th}})^2}
  \;-\; (R_a + R_{\mathrm{th}}).
\label{eq:Rfhif_exact}
\end{equation}
When $\Rf \gg X_d'' + X_{\mathrm{th}}$, \eqref{eq:Rfhif_exact} simplifies to
\begin{equation}
R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}} \approx \frac{\sqrt{2}\,V_0}{I_{\mathrm{inst}}}
  - (R_a + R_{\mathrm{th}}).
\label{eq:Rfhif}
\end{equation}
The no-trip boundary is analogously found from $I_{\mathrm{rms}} = I_p$:
\begin{equation}
R_f^{\mathrm{NT}} =
  \sqrt{\!\left(\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}\,I_p}\right)^{\!2}
  - (X_d'' + X_{\mathrm{th}})^2}
  \;-\; (R_a + R_{\mathrm{th}}).
\label{eq:RfNT}
\end{equation}
The HIF inverse-time window spans
$\Rf \in [R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}},\, R_f^{\mathrm{NT}}]$.

\subsection{Regime Boundaries for the Study System}
\label{sec:regime:study}

Using $Z_d'' + Z_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.02 + j0.30\pu$ and base impedance
$Z_B = V_B^2/S_B = 33^2/10 = 108.9\,\Omega \approx 121\,\Omega$ (machine base):
\begin{align}
R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}} &= \sqrt{(0.50)^2 - (0.30)^2} - 0.02
   = 0.40 - 0.02 = 0.38\pu \approx 46\,\Omega, \label{eq:Rfhif_num} \\
R_f^{\mathrm{NT}}  &= \sqrt{(0.884)^2 - (0.30)^2} - 0.02
   = 0.835 - 0.02 = 0.815\pu \approx 98\,\Omega. \label{eq:RfNT_num}
\end{align}
The 3PH HIF inverse-time window is therefore $\Rf \in [46,\,98]\,\Omega$,
a 52~$\Omega$ range.  For SLG faults, the effective $M$ is approximately
60\% of the 3PH value (reduced positive-sequence contribution), so the
window closes near $R_f \approx 40\,\Omega$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map}).

\section{Baseline Relay Law and Its Limitation Under HIF}
\label{sec:baseline}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{IEC Very-Inverse Characteristic}
\label{sec:baseline:vi}

The IEC~60255-151 very-inverse (VI) time-current characteristic is
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{op}}(M) = \frac{\tms \times k_1}{M^\alpha - 1},
\qquad M > 1,
\label{eq:tVI}
\end{equation}
with VI constants $k_1 = 13.5$, $\alpha = 1.0$ (IEC standard
curve type~B)~\cite{IEC60255-151}.
The partial derivative with respect to TMS is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial t_{\mathrm{op}}}{\partial\,\tms}
  = \frac{k_1}{M - 1},
\label{eq:dtdTMS}
\end{equation}
which grows without bound as $M \to 1^+$.  This makes TMS the decisive
setting in the inverse-time regime.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis at HIF Levels}
\label{sec:baseline:sensitivity}

For the study system with $I_p = 0.8\pu$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bolted 3PH fault} ($\Rf = 0$, $M = 2.94$):
        $t_{\mathrm{op}} = 0.12 \times 13.5/1.94 = 0.835\sss$.
        A 30\% reduction in TMS (from 0.12 to 0.084) saves only 0.25\,s.

  \item \textbf{Moderate HIF} ($\Rf = 72\,\Omega$, $M = 1.31$):
        $t_{\mathrm{op}} = 0.12 \times 13.5/0.31 = 5.22\sss$.
        A 30\% TMS reduction to $\tmss = 0.084$ gives $3.65\sss$ --- a
        \textbf{30\%} improvement.  A 50\% reduction to $\tmss = 0.06$
        gives $2.61\sss$ (\textbf{50\%} improvement).

  \item \textbf{Deep HIF} ($\Rf = 90\,\Omega$, $M = 1.10$):
        $t_{\mathrm{op}} = 0.12 \times 13.5/0.10 = 16.2\sss$.
        A 50\% TMS reduction to 0.06 gives $8.1\sss$ --- still slow, but
        halved.  The relay is near the no-trip boundary.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The root cause is structural: $\partial t_{\mathrm{op}}/\partial\tms$ is
inversely proportional to $(M-1)$, which collapses near the pickup boundary.
A single fixed TMS cannot simultaneously provide fast clearance at high $M$
(needs small TMS) and fast clearance at HIF $M$ levels.

\section{Retained Inverse-Estimation Framework}
\label{sec:estimation}
% =============================================================================

The Stage~1 estimation framework~\cite{Eluvathingal2025_TR01} is retained
without modification.  This section summarises its elements for completeness
and self-consistency; full derivations are in~\cite{Eluvathingal2025_TR01}.

\subsection{Two-Pass Levenberg--Marquardt Estimator}
\label{sec:estimation:lm}

Parameters are identified by minimising the sum of squared residuals:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bm{\theta}} = \arg\min_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta}
  \left\|\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta})\right\|_2^2
  = \arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N} \bigl[i_a(k) - f(\bm{\theta}, k)\bigr]^2,
\label{eq:lsq}
\end{equation}
where $f(\bm{\theta}, k)$ is the model~\eqref{eq:ia} evaluated at sample $k$,
and $\Theta$ is the parameter feasibility set with physical bounds.

The LM update at iteration $\ell$ is
\begin{equation}
\bm{\theta}^{(\ell+1)} = \bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}
  + \bigl(\mat{J}^{(\ell)\top}\mat{J}^{(\ell)}
           + \mu^{(\ell)}\mat{I}\bigr)^{-1}
    \mat{J}^{(\ell)\top}\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}),
\label{eq:lm_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mat{J}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times 6}$ is the Jacobian
$[\partial f/\partial\theta_i]$ evaluated analytically, and $\mu^{(\ell)} > 0$
is the LM damping parameter updated by the Levenberg--Marquardt trust-region
rule~\cite{Marquardt1963}.

\textbf{Analytical Jacobian.}  The non-zero columns of $\mat{J}$ are:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial i_a}{\partial t_0}
  &= -\frac{(I_{sub}-I_{ss})}{\tau_{ac}}\,
     e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{ac}}\sin(\omega t+\phi_a)
     \nonumber\\
  &\quad + \frac{I_{sub}\sin\phi_a}{\tau_{dc}}\,e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{dc}},
\label{eq:J_t0}\\
\frac{\partial i_a}{\partial I_{ss}}
  &= \bigl(1 - e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{ac}}\bigr)\sin(\omega t+\phi_a),
\label{eq:J_Iss}\\
\frac{\partial i_a}{\partial I_{sub}}
  &= e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{ac}}\sin(\omega t+\phi_a)
     - \sin\phi_a\,e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{dc}},
\label{eq:J_Isub}\\
\frac{\partial i_a}{\partial \tau_{ac}}
  &= \frac{(I_{sub}-I_{ss})\,t_{\mathrm{rel}}}{\tau_{ac}^2}\,
     e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{ac}}\sin(\omega t+\phi_a),
\label{eq:J_tauac}\\
\frac{\partial i_a}{\partial \tau_{dc}}
  &= -\frac{I_{sub}\sin\phi_a\,t_{\mathrm{rel}}}{\tau_{dc}^2}\,
     e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{dc}},
\label{eq:J_taudc}\\
\frac{\partial i_a}{\partial \phi_a}
  &= \bigl[I_{ss}+(I_{sub}-I_{ss})e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{ac}}\bigr]
     \cos(\omega t+\phi_a)
     \nonumber\\
  &\quad - I_{sub}\cos\phi_a\,e^{-t_{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau_{dc}}.
\label{eq:J_phia}
\end{align}

\textbf{Two-pass strategy.}  A single-pass LM over all six parameters is
ill-conditioned because $I_{ss}$ and $\tau_{ac}$ are masked by the
large DC transient in the early fault window.  The two-pass approach
resolves this:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pass~1} (full window $[t_0,\,t_0+W_1]$): estimates
        $[t_0, I_{sub}, \phi_a, \tau_{dc}]$ with $I_{ss}$ and
        $\tau_{ac}$ fixed at nominal.
  \item \textbf{Pass~2} (tail window $[t_0+3\tau_{dc},\,t_0+W_2]$,
        after DC has decayed to $< e^{-3} \approx 5\%$): refines
        $[I_{ss},\tau_{ac}]$ with all other parameters fixed at
        Pass-1 estimates.
\end{itemize}
The tail-window selection ensures the DC exponential has decayed
sufficiently that $I_{ss}$ and $\tau_{ac}$ are independently identifiable
from the AC waveform shape.  Fig.~\ref{fig:lm_flowchart} shows the complete
algorithm.

\subsection{Identifiability Measure: Condition Number}
\label{sec:estimation:identifiability}

The normalised condition number of the Gauss--Newton information matrix
\begin{equation}
\kappan =
  \frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mat{J}^\top\mat{J})}
       {\sigma_{\min}(\mat{J}^\top\mat{J})}
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
quantifies whether the local parameter manifold is well-conditioned.
When $\kappan$ is large, the LM solution is non-unique and should not
be accepted.  The column-normalised form prevents large-magnitude parameters
from dominating the singular-value spectrum.

\subsection{Composite Confidence Score}
\label{sec:estimation:confidence}

Three sub-scores are combined:
\begin{align}
\gamma_\kappa   &= \operatorname{clip}\!\left(
                   1 - \frac{\kappan}{\kappa_{\max}},\; 0,\; 1\right),
\label{eq:gamma_kappa}\\
\gamma_\varepsilon &= \operatorname{clip}\!\left(
                   1 - \frac{\|\mathbf{r}\|_2}{\varepsilon_{\max}},\; 0,\; 1\right),
\label{eq:gamma_eps}\\
\gamma_n        &= \min\!\left(\frac{n_c}{n_{c,\min}},\, 1\right),
\label{eq:gamma_n}
\end{align}
where $n_c$ is the number of consecutive accepted cycles.  The composite
score is the weighted combination
\begin{equation}
\gamma = w_1\,\gamma_\kappa + w_2\,\gamma_\varepsilon + w_3\,\gamma_n,
\quad w_1 = w_2 = 0.40,\; w_3 = 0.20.
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
Parameters: $\kappa_{\max} = 20$, $\varepsilon_{\max} = 0.15\pu$,
$n_{c,\min} = 3$.
Adaptive TMS updates are accepted when $\gamma \geq \gammin = 0.80$.

\subsection{Bounded Update Law}
\label{sec:estimation:bounded}

The TMS is updated by:
\begin{equation}
\tms_{k+1} = \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(\tms_k +
              \alpha\,\Delta\tms,\;
              \tms_{\min},\; \tms_{\max}\bigr),
\label{eq:bounded}
\end{equation}
with rate limit $|\Delta\tms| \leq \Delta_{\max}$ per cycle, and
$\alpha = 0.30$ (step-size gain).  Bounds are $\tms_{\min} = 0.05$,
$\tms_{\max} = 0.25$, $\Delta_{\max} = 0.02$.  These ensure:
(i)~the TMS never falls below the minimum grading margin value;
(ii)~the adaptation is step-free and protection-grade monotone.

\section{Impedance-Aware Adaptive TMS Law}
\label{sec:tmslaw}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Stage~1 Baseline Mapping and Its HIF Deficiency}
\label{sec:tmslaw:baseline}

Stage~1~\cite{Eluvathingal2025_TR01} derived a linear mapping
\begin{equation}
\tmss = K_{\mathrm{TMS}}\,\tauac, \qquad K_{\mathrm{TMS}} = 0.12,
\label{eq:tms_s1}
\end{equation}
where $\tauac$ is the LM-estimated AC envelope time constant.  This mapping
sets TMS proportional to the fault-current decay rate, which correctly
scales with the machine's transient time constant $T_{d0}'$.  However,
\eqref{eq:tms_s1} is \emph{invariant} to $\Rf$: it does not reduce TMS
when the fault current multiple $M$ is depressed by high impedance.

\begin{remark}[Stage~1 deficiency in the HIF regime]
For $\tauac = 0.83\sss$ (typical), Stage~1 gives $\tmss = 0.10$.
At $M = 2.94$ (bolted): $t_{\mathrm{op}} = 0.62\sss$.
At $M = 1.31$ (HIF, $\Rf = 72\,\Omega$): $t_{\mathrm{op}} = 4.35\sss$.
The 7$\times$ increase in operate time occurs because Stage~1 treats
both operating points identically.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Resistance-Aware Correction Factor}
\label{sec:tmslaw:factor}

From~\eqref{eq:Iss}, $I_{ss}$ decreases monotonically with $\Rf$
and can therefore serve as a continuous proxy for the HIF severity
without requiring explicit fault-type classification.
Define the normalised correction factor
\begin{equation}
\fhif(\Iss) = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(
  \frac{\Iss}{I_{ss}^{\mathrm{nom}}},\; f_{\min},\; 1.0\right),
\label{eq:fhif}
\end{equation}
where $I_{ss}^{\mathrm{nom}} = V_0/(X_d + X_{\mathrm{th}}) = 1.0/1.95 = 0.513\pu$
is the bolted-fault steady-state reference, and $f_{\min} = 0.30$ is the
lower clip preventing extreme TMS reduction.  The clip $f_{\min} = 0.30$
ensures $\tmss \geq K_{\mathrm{TMS}}^{\mathrm{eff}} \times 0.83 \times 0.30$,
which after the bounded update maintains the minimum grading margin
$\tms_{\min}$.

\subsection{Regime-Dependent Effective Gain}
\label{sec:tmslaw:gain}

The effective gain is defined as
\begin{equation}
\Keff =
\begin{cases}
  K_{\mathrm{TMS}} = 0.12 & \text{if } \fhif \geq f_{\mathrm{sw}} = 0.90,\\[2pt]
  K_{\mathrm{TMS}}^{\mathrm{HIF}} = 0.08 & \text{if } \fhif < f_{\mathrm{sw}}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:Keff}
\end{equation}
The threshold $f_{\mathrm{sw}} = 0.90$ corresponds to
$\Iss = 0.90 \times 0.513 = 0.46\pu$,
which occurs near $\Rf = R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}} = 46\,\Omega$ for the study system.
Below this threshold both $\fhif$ and $\Keff$ are reduced, producing a
compounded TMS reduction:
\begin{equation}
\text{HIF regime}: \quad
\tmss = 0.08 \times \tauac \times \fhif.
\label{eq:tms_hif}
\end{equation}
The reduction in $\Keff$ from 0.12 to 0.08 (33\% reduction) is justified
by the requirement that $\tmss$ must remain above $\tms_{\min} = 0.05$ even
at the maximum permitted $\fhif$ reduction (i.e., when $\fhif = f_{\min} = 0.30$).

\subsection{Extended Adaptive TMS Law}
\label{sec:tmslaw:law}

The complete impedance-aware adaptive TMS law is:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\tmss = \Keff(\fhif)\;\cdot\;\tauac\;\cdot\;\fhif(\Iss)}
\label{eq:tms_ext}
\end{equation}
This is the central result of the paper.  It modifies the Stage~1 mapping
by:
(i)~multiplying by the monotonically decreasing correction factor
$\fhif(\Iss)$ that continuously tracks HIF severity; and
(ii)~switching the effective gain $\Keff$ when the operating point crosses
into the HIF regime.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\scalebox{0.82}{%
\input{figures/fig_tms_blockdiagram}%
}
\caption{Block diagram of the impedance-aware adaptive TMS law.  The two-pass
  LM estimator feeds $\Iss$ and $\tauac$ to the correction factor and
  gain-switch blocks.  Their product is rate-limited and clipped before
  updating the OC relay.  The confidence gate ($\gamma \geq 0.80$) enables
  the update only when the estimate is reliable.}
\label{fig:tms_blockdiagram}
\end{figure}

\begin{proposition}[TMS Reduction Under HIF]
\label{prop:tmsreduce}
For any fault in the HIF regime ($\fhif < 0.90$) with representative
$\tauac = 0.83\sss$, the extended law~\eqref{eq:tms_ext} gives
\begin{equation}
\tmss = 0.08 \times 0.83 \times \fhif = 0.0664\,\fhif.
\label{eq:prop_tms}
\end{equation}
Since $\fhif \leq 0.90 < 1.0$:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item $\tmss \leq 0.060$, representing at least a \textbf{40\% reduction}
        relative to the Stage~1 baseline $\tmss_0 = 0.12 \times 0.83 = 0.10$.
  \item After the lower clip $\tms_{\min} = 0.05$, the operate time at
        $M = 1.31$ (moderate HIF) reduces from $5.22\sss$
        (fixed $\tms = 0.12$) to at most $2.10\sss$ --- a \textbf{60\%}
        reduction in clearance time.
  \item The grading margin at the no-trip boundary ($M \to 1^+$) is
        preserved because the bounded update~\eqref{eq:bounded} ensures
        $\tms \geq \tms_{\min}$ at all times.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\textit{Proof.}
Substituting $\Keff = 0.08$ and $\tauac = 0.83$ into~\eqref{eq:tms_ext}:
$\tmss = 0.08 \times 0.83 \times \fhif = 0.0664\,\fhif$.
For $\fhif \in [0.30, 0.90)$, $\tmss \in [0.0199, 0.0598)$.
After the clip in~\eqref{eq:bounded}: $\tms \geq 0.05$.
Operate time: $t_{\mathrm{op}}^{\mathrm{adapt}} = \tms \times 13.5/(M-1)
\leq 0.060 \times 13.5/(M-1)$, compared with
$t_{\mathrm{op}}^{\mathrm{fixed}} = 0.12 \times 13.5/(M-1)$.
The ratio is $\leq 0.060/0.12 = 0.50$.  At the clip ($\tms_{\min} = 0.05$),
the ratio is $0.05/0.12 = 0.417$. $\square$

\begin{remark}[Gain-switch design]
The threshold $f_{\mathrm{sw}} = 0.90$ represents a conservative engineering
choice to avoid TMS over-reduction near the HIF boundary ($\fhif \approx 0.92$)
where estimation may be marginally uncertain.  A continuous interpolation
$K(\fhif) = 0.08 + 0.04(\fhif - 0.30)/0.60$ is an alternative that avoids
the gain discontinuity.  This is deferred to future work.
\end{remark}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{What the Paper Establishes}

The analysis and results support three claims:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Adaptive benefit is strictly confined to the inverse-time regime.}
      In the instantaneous region ($\Rf < 46\,\Omega$), both fixed and
      adaptive TMS produce identical clearance times (the instantaneous element
      dominates; TMS is irrelevant).  The transition at $R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}}$
      is sharp, as predicted analytically.  This closes the main limitation
      of Stage~1 by confirming where TMS adaptation matters.

\item \textbf{The law responds to estimated physics, not a pre-classified
      fault type.}  The correction factor $\fhif(\Iss)$ decreases
      continuously as $\Rf$ increases~(Fig.~\ref{fig:est_params}),
      producing a graded TMS reduction over the entire HIF window.
      No explicit fault-type signal or external measurement is required.

\item \textbf{Security is maintained throughout.}
      Zero false trips across 144 cases.  The confidence gate correctly
      blocks adaptation near the no-trip boundary (low $\gamma$), preventing
      the relay from issuing too-fast a trip at depressed current near $M = 1$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Inherent Protection Limits of Adaptive OC Under HIF}

Adaptive TMS \emph{cannot} extend the OC operable range beyond the no-trip
boundary.  From~\eqref{eq:RfNT}, $R_f^{\mathrm{NT}}$ is determined solely
by $I_p$ and the system impedance; it is independent of TMS.  For
$\Rf > R_f^{\mathrm{NT}}$, the relay does not pick up regardless of TMS.
The adaptive law can only improve clearance speed within the window
$[R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}},\,R_f^{\mathrm{NT}}]$.

For HIFs outside this range, complementary schemes are required:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Directional earth-fault (DEF):} detects zero-sequence current
        independent of OC pickup level.
  \item \textbf{Restricted earth-fault (REF):} uses transformer neutral
        current; detects $\Rf$ up to several hundred ohms.
  \item \textbf{Waveform/spectral methods:} harmonic signature detection,
        wavelet-based HIF detection~\cite{Lazkano2000,Zamanan2019}.
\end{itemize}
These elements are complementary to the adaptive OC scheme,
not competitive.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Extensions}

\begin{description}
\item[Sequence-component adaptation:]
     Phase-A estimation is used throughout.  For SLG faults,
     Hilbert--Fortescue sequence decomposition would improve $\Iss$
     identifiability from the negative- and zero-sequence components,
     potentially widening the SLG operative window.

\item[Long-window AVR dynamics:]
     Model~\eqref{eq:ia} captures the first 150--300~ms faithfully but
     diverges at longer windows due to AVR excitation dynamics.  For
     inverse-time accumulation over 5--15~s (deep HIF near the no-trip
     boundary), the sixth-order Park $dq$ model may be required for
     accurate online estimation.

\item[Multi-generator grading:]
     Independent adaptive TMS reduction by parallel generators can violate
     time-grading margins.  Centralised selectivity enforcement is outside
     this paper's scope.

\item[Hardware-in-loop validation:]
     RTDS / OPAL-RT injection tests connected to a hardware numerical relay
     via IEC~61850 sampled-value streams are required before field deployment.
\end{description}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper has extended confidence-gated inverse-estimation-based adaptive
OC protection from the instantaneous regime into the inverse-time HIF regime
by deriving an impedance-aware adaptive TMS law.  Three conclusions are
supported:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{HIF pushes OC operation into the inverse-time regime.}
      When $\Rf > R_f^{\mathrm{HIF}}$, the instantaneous element is inactive
      and clearance time is governed entirely by the TMS.  A fixed TMS
      calibrated for low-impedance faults produces multi-second delays
      in this regime, with operate time growing as $\sim 1/(M-1)$.

\item \textbf{Online inverse estimation can drive a meaningful adaptive TMS law.}
      The estimated steady-state current $\Iss$ provides a continuous,
      physics-based proxy for fault resistance.  The correction factor
      $\fhif$ and regime-dependent gain $\Keff$ together produce a graded
      TMS reduction in the HIF window, with confidence acceptance
      exceeding \textbf{93\%} in the operative range.

\item \textbf{The adaptive method improves clearing time while preserving
      bounded, confidence-gated relay behaviour.}
      Trip-time reductions of \textbf{21--53\%} at moderate HIF and
      up to \textbf{53\%} at deep HIF are demonstrated, with zero
      false trips and correct non-operation at the analytical no-trip
      boundary.
\end{enumerate}

The framework establishes that inverse estimation delivers a tangible
protection benefit specifically in the inverse-time regime, where HIF
makes TMS adaptation decisive.  Real-time hardware validation and
extension to multi-generator coordination are the natural next steps.
